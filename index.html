Research

My research centers on sustainable and environmentally friendly approaches to plant disease control. Rather than depending on synthetic chemical fumigants and fungicides, which can harm soil health and beneficial organisms over time, I look for natural or biological alternatives that protect crops while preserving the ecosystems they grow in. In the projects below, I draw on my training in plant pathology, molecular biology, and crop science to tackle three interconnected problems: how to make biological soil fumigants more effective, how to shield grapevines from the trunk diseases that shorten their productive lives, and how plants negotiate their partnerships with soil fungi. Each project is presented with a short summary, a supporting figure, and a link to the full paper wherever one has been published.

Dissertation · in progress

Bio-fumigation 2.0: Engineering Bacteria to Supercharge Brassica Fumigants

PhD, University of Idaho — Dr. Eric Holmes & Dr. Klas Udekwu

Brassica juncea seed meal releases natural fumigant compounds (isothiocyanates) only when an enzyme called myrosinase is present. Because myrosinase and its substrates are normally kept apart in the plant, their fumigant yield in soil is limited. I am engineering Pseudomonas putida to express and secrete myrosinase directly into the soil, so the enzyme can act on mustard seed-meal extract where it’s applied. My aim is to boost the isothiocyanate concentration enough to control nematodes, offering a renewable alternative to synthetic soil fumigants.

Research · ongoing

Inside the Partnership: Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi & Legumes

Faculty Research Assistant, Oregon State University — Dr. Sergey Ivanov

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) form a mutualistic partnership with most crop roots, helping plants absorb phosphorus in exchange for sugars. Using Medicago truncatula, I studied the molecular and cellular machinery that governs this symbiosis. I performed Agrobacterium-mediated hairy-root and stable transformation, generated transgenic lines by Gateway cloning and CRISPR-Cas9 multiplex gene editing, made point mutants by overlapping PCR, and characterized AMF root-length colonization and transgenic genotypes to reveal how the plant regulates fungal entry.

MS thesis · published

Protecting Grapevine Pruning Wounds from Trunk Disease

MS, Plant Pathology, Oregon State University (SOREC) — Dr. Achala N. KC

Grapevine trunk disease (GTD) enters through pruning wounds and can weaken or kill vines. I led field trials in two Oregon vineyards to test how well pruning-wound protectants and copper-based fungicides prevent Botryosphaeria dieback, and how long that protection lasts. Across both conventional and organic systems I compared single versus repeated fungicide applications and assessed residual protection over the dormant season. The results inform timing and frequency recommendations that growers can use to cut back trunk-disease losses.
